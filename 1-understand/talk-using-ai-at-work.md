# Talk: using AI well at work

> **In one minute**
> - AI chat tools predict likely words, so a fluent, confident answer can still be wrong.
> - They can speed up everyday work such as drafting, but can make results worse on tasks just beyond what they do well.
> - Whoever sends the work owns it, so check what matters and open any source it cites.
> - Keep sensitive information out of public AI tools, and use the tools your organisation has approved.
> - Try one routine task with an approved tool this week, check the result, and share what you found.

*Reading time: about 10 minutes · Best for: presenting to colleagues new to AI*

A talk for colleagues in any role who are new to using AI tools at work: 12 slides, 16:9, for about 25 minutes plus questions. It is about using the tools people already have, well and safely, not about building with AI, and the aim is that everyone leaves with one habit to try the following week. This outline holds the slide text, the speaker notes and the sources; the deck itself is `talk-using-ai-at-work.pptx` in this folder. Numbers in square brackets point to the Sources list at the end. ## Slide 2 · Basics · What these tools do

**On the slide**

- They predict the most likely words, from training and your prompt [1]
- Guidance for judges: that is "not necessarily the most accurate answer" [1]
- Their knowledge is typically fixed after training, plus what you give them [2]

**Key line:** Treat a fluent answer as a first draft, not a fact.

**Speaker notes**

Guidance for judges in England and Wales puts it plainly: public AI chatbots "do not provide answers from authoritative databases", but generate new text based on the prompts they receive and the data they were trained on.[1]
A prompt is simply what you type in: in the guidance's words, "an input or instruction given to an AI system".[1]
The output is what the model predicts to be the most likely combination of words, which the guidance says "is not necessarily the most accurate answer".[1]
The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) describes the same thing: the model turns the relationships between words into a probability model, and answers a prompt from those relationships.[2]
It adds that the data in the model is typically fixed once it has been trained, although it can be refined, for example by giving it context information with the question.[2]

## Slide 3 · Basics · Why they sound sure

**On the slide**

- OpenAI defines hallucination as a confident answer that isn't true [3]
- It argues training and tests reward guessing over admitting uncertainty [3]
- Like a multiple-choice test: a guess might score; a blank never does [3]
- Asked for one author's PhD thesis title: three confident, wrong answers [3]

**Key line:** Judge an answer by checking it, never by how sure it sounds.

**Speaker notes**

OpenAI calls hallucinations "plausible but false statements generated by language models": instances where a model confidently generates an answer that isn't true.[3]
When OpenAI asked a widely used chatbot for the title of the PhD dissertation of one of the paper's authors, it confidently produced three different answers, none of them correct.[3]
OpenAI's own account, set out in a research paper, argues that the standard ways models are trained and tested reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty.[3]
Its analogy is a multiple-choice test: a wild guess might get lucky, but leaving it blank guarantees a zero, so a model graded only on accuracy is encouraged to guess rather than say "I don't know".[3]
OpenAI also says hallucinations remain a fundamental challenge for every model of this kind, and that it is working hard to reduce them further.[3]

## Slide 4 · Where it helps most

**On the slide**

- Support agents resolved 15% more issues an hour, on average, with AI [4]
- Less experienced, lower-skilled agents improved in both speed and quality [4]
- The most experienced and skilled: small speed gains, small quality declines [4]
- In one writing experiment, average time fell 40% and quality rose 18% [5]

**Key line:** Start with routine drafting and replies, and check what it gives you.

**Speaker notes**

A Stanford and MIT study followed 5,172 customer-support agents as an AI chat assistant was introduced to them in stages.[4]
On average, agents with access to it resolved 15% more issues per hour.[4]
Less experienced and lower-skilled agents improved in both speed and quality, while the most experienced and highest-skilled saw small gains in speed and small declines in quality.[4]
In a separate experiment, published in Science, 453 college-educated professionals did writing tasks drawn from their own occupations, and half of them were given ChatGPT.[5]
With it, the average time taken fell by 40% and output quality rose by 18%, and inequality between workers decreased.[5]

## Slide 5 · Uneven ability: the jagged frontier

**On the slide**

- Harvard study of 758 consultants, on tasks either side of AI's limits [6]
- Inside: on average 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, better quality [6]
- Outside: about 84.5% right without AI; 60% and 70.6% with it [6]
- Even wrong answers from AI users were more coherent and persuasive [6]

**Key line:** Before relying on it, try it on a task where you know the answer.

**Speaker notes**

A Harvard study, run with Boston Consulting Group, randomly gave 758 of the firm's consultants access to AI or none, on realistic consulting tasks.[6]
The authors call the edge of what AI does well a "jagged technological frontier": AI helps on some tasks and makes things worse on others that seem just as hard.[6]
On 18 tasks inside the frontier, those using AI completed 12.2% more tasks and finished them 25.1% more quickly on average, with significantly better quality.[6]
On one task chosen to sit outside it, about 84.5% of those without AI got it right, against 60% and 70.6% in the two AI groups.[6]
Even there, the AI users' recommendations were rated more coherent and persuasive, which the authors say shows that AI "improves presentation and argumentation even when the underlying analysis is flawed".[6]

## Slide 6 · Confident, wrong and in court

**On the slide**

- High Court, 2025: fake citations from actual or suspected AI use [7]
- One case: 18 of 45 citations named cases that don't exist [7]
- The court: such tools may give "confident assertions that are simply untrue" [7]
- Lawyers' duty: check AI research against authoritative sources before using it [7]

**Key line:** If it cites a source, open the source before you use it.

**Speaker notes**

In a judgment of June 2025, the High Court in London dealt with two cases in which false information, typically a fake citation or quotation, had reached the court through lawyers' actual or suspected use of generative AI tools without checking.[7]
In one of them, the list of references held 45 citations, and in 18 of them the case cited did not exist.[7]
The client said the citations came from publicly available AI tools, legal search engines and online sources, and accepted that his confidence in them had been misplaced.[7]
The court warned that freely available AI tools "can produce apparently coherent and plausible responses to prompts, but those coherent and plausible responses may turn out to be entirely incorrect".[7]
It said those who use AI for legal research have a professional duty to check it against authoritative sources before using it, and that a lawyer is not entitled to rely on a lay client for the accuracy of citations.[7]

## Slide 7 · You own what it says

**On the slide**

- Air Canada's chatbot suggested a bereavement fare could be claimed after travel [8]
- The airline's own policy page said no claims after travel [8]
- Air Canada argued it wasn't liable; the tribunal: "a remarkable submission" [8]
- Ruling: it is responsible for all the information on its website [8]

**Key line:** Whatever tool drafted it, whoever sends it owns it.

**Speaker notes**

British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal decided this case in February 2024: a customer booking flights after a grandmother's death had used a chatbot on Air Canada's website, and Air Canada gave the tribunal no information about the nature of that chatbot.[8]
The chatbot suggested a reduced bereavement fare could be claimed retroactively, but Air Canada's bereavement travel page said the policy did not apply after travel was completed.[8]
Air Canada argued it could not be held liable for information from its agents or representatives, including a chatbot, and the tribunal called the effect of that argument "a remarkable submission".[8]
It found Air Canada had not taken reasonable care to ensure its chatbot was accurate: "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."[8]
Air Canada was ordered to pay a total of $812.02 in damages, interest and fees.[8]

## Slide 8 · What you paste in goes somewhere

**On the slide**

- NCSC: what you type is visible to the organisation providing the tool [2]
- It recommends keeping sensitive information out of public AI tools [2]
- Judges' guidance: see public chatbot input as "published to all the world" [1]
- Use your organisation's approved tools, and follow its rules

**Key line:** If you wouldn't publish it, don't paste it into a public tool.

**Speaker notes**

The UK's NCSC points out that whatever you type into a public AI tool will be visible to the organisation providing it, and that those queries are stored and will almost certainly be used to develop the service or model at some point.[2]
It adds that a question can be sensitive because of who is asking it, not only what it contains, and that stored queries could be hacked, leaked or, more likely, accidentally made public.[2]
So it recommends leaving sensitive information out of queries to public tools, and not asking anything that would cause problems if it were made public.[2]
The guidance for judges in England and Wales goes further: anything put into a public AI chatbot "should be seen as being published to all the world".[1]
The last bullet is advice: use the tools your organisation has approved, on work devices, and follow its rules on what may go in.

## Slide 9 · Keep your own judgement

**On the slide**

- Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon: a survey of 319 knowledge workers [9]
- Those more confident in AI reported less critical thinking [9]
- Those confident they could do the task themselves reported more [9]
- Thinking shifts to checking, fitting answers in, and overseeing the task [9]

**Key line:** The more you trust it, the more deliberately you should check.

**Speaker notes**

Researchers at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University surveyed 319 knowledge workers, who shared 936 first-hand examples of using generative AI in their work.[9]
Those more confident in the AI's ability to do a task reported less critical thinking, while those more confident they could do the task themselves reported more.[9]
The authors found that AI shifts the nature of critical thinking towards verifying information, integrating the AI's response into the work, and stewarding the task.[9]
They also warn that it may reduce critical engagement, particularly in routine or lower-stakes tasks in which people simply rely on the AI.[9]
These are self-reports, and the authors note that their sample leaned towards younger, more technologically skilled people who use AI at work at least once a week.[9]

## Slide 10 · Three habits that work

*Layout: three*

**On the slide**

- Brief it: the purpose, the audience and the facts it needs
- Check it: open its sources; check harder when more is at stake
- Own it: if it goes out under your name, it's yours

**Speaker notes**

These three habits are advice, not findings from any one study.
Brief it as you would a capable new colleague: what the work is for, who will read it, and the facts it needs, taken from approved sources.
Guidance for judges notes that the quality of any answer depends on how you engage with the tool, including the prompts you enter.[1]
Check in proportion to the stakes, and open every source it cites: the same guidance warns that cited source material might also be hallucinated, which it defines as incorrect or misleading results that AI models generate.[1]
Own it: whatever drafted the work, the person who sends it answers for it.

## Slide 11 · Try this week

*Layout: discussion*

**On the slide**

- This week: one routine task, an approved tool, and a careful check
- Pick a task you know well, so you can spot mistakes
- Afterwards, tell your team what worked and what it got wrong

**Key line:** Choose a task where a mistake is cheap and easy to spot.

**Speaker notes**

This is the one habit to try next week: take one routine task, do it with a tool your organisation has approved, and check the result properly.
Pick something you know well, so you can tell when it's wrong, and where a mistake would be cheap and easy to put right.
Guidance for judges lists tasks like these as potentially useful, among them summarising large bodies of text, taking care that the summary is accurate, and composing, summarising and prioritising emails.[1]
Keep sensitive information out unless the tool is approved for it, and skip anything your organisation says AI must never be used for.
Then tell your team what worked and what it got wrong, so everyone learns from it.
