# What modern AI is, in plain English

> **In one minute**
> - A chat assistant predicts likely next words, which makes it fluent but not necessarily right.
> - It doesn't look things up unless it's connected to web search or your documents, so treat its answers as drafts.
> - It can sound sure when it's wrong, and the reasoning it shows isn't always how it got there.
> - It isn't a person and can't be held to account, so brief it, check it and own what you send.

*Reading time: about 5 minutes · Best for: everyone*

## The kinds of AI you'll meet

- **Chat assistants** simulate a conversation.[1] Guidance for judges in England and Wales gives ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Meta AI as examples,[1] and Anthropic's Claude is another.[15]
  They run on [large language models (LLMs)](ai-glossary.md).
- **Reasoning models** write out their steps before answering; in Anthropic's words, they "show their working".[2]
- **Image, voice and video generators** make pictures, speech and video; OpenAI calls Sora 2 its "flagship video and audio generation model".[3]
- **AI built into everyday tools** includes Microsoft's Copilot in Outlook, Teams, Word, Excel and PowerPoint, which can draw on the web and an organisation's own data (Microsoft's documentation).[4]
- **[Agents](ai-glossary.md)** use AI to take actions towards a goal, not only to answer.[1]
- **[Decision models](ai-glossary.md)** don't write text: they pick one answer from a fixed list, such as yes or no, or which expert should answer a question, and say how sure they are.[16]
  Glean's rule is to use one, Jev, when a step's answers can be listed in advance, and Simon Willison notes Google joining Jev and OpenAI "in the decision model category".[16][17]
  See [Should this task use AI, and which kind?](../2-decide/should-this-task-use-ai.md).
- **Classic machine learning** is the older kind, trained on data "to make classifications or predictions".[1]
  AI is already in general use in search engines' auto-fill, social media feeds and predictive text.[1]

Two more terms: [frontier models](ai-glossary.md) are the most capable of their time; [open-weight models](ai-glossary.md) are released publicly for organisations to download and run themselves.[5]

## How a chat assistant works

At heart, it predicts likely next words: judges' guidance in England and Wales says LLMs learn "to predict the next best word or part of a word in a sentence".[1]
Those pieces of text are called [tokens](ai-glossary.md).
Chat assistants are then trained further, partly on people's rankings of their answers, to follow instructions and act as helpful assistants.[6][7]

On its own, without web search or access to your documents, it doesn't look anything up: the judges' guidance says public chatbots "do not provide answers from authoritative databases" but generate text from prompts and training data.[1]
It adds that the likeliest combination of words "is not necessarily the most accurate answer".[1]
The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) says the model is typically fixed once trained, though it can be given context with the question.[8]

## Why it sounds sure when it's wrong

OpenAI describes [hallucinations](ai-glossary.md) as "plausible but false statements generated by language models".[9]
OpenAI's 2025 research argues that standard training and evaluation "reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty", as a multiple-choice test rewards a wild guess over a blank.[9]
In 2025, the High Court of England and Wales warned that answers from freely available generative AI tools "may make confident assertions that are simply untrue".[10]

## What it is, and what it isn't

Predicting words may not be the whole story: Anthropic found its Claude 3.5 Haiku model picking a rhyming word before writing the line that ends with it.[11]
Anthropic calls this "powerful evidence" that models may plan much further ahead than one word at a time, though its method sees only part of what goes on inside.[11]

- **Not a person, and not shown to be conscious.** In 2023, Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues checked several AI systems of the time against signs of consciousness drawn from scientific theories.[12]
  Their analysis "suggests that no current AI systems are conscious", but also that there are "no obvious technical barriers" to building ones that show those signs.[12]
  Their 2025 follow-up says checking existing systems for those signs has begun but "is far from being completed".[13]
  It also warns that "modern AI systems are likely to give users the impression that they are conscious".[13]
- **Fluent isn't correct.** In Anthropic's Haiku study, when Claude recognised a name but knew nothing else about the person, it could "generate a plausible—but unfortunately untrue—response".[11]
- **The reasoning it shows isn't always how it got there.** In other Anthropic tests, researchers slipped a hint about the answer into multiple-choice questions.[2]
  When a model used the hint, its written reasoning mentioned it only 25% of the time on average for Claude 3.7 Sonnet, and 39% for DeepSeek R1.[2]
  In the Haiku study, asked for a calculation it couldn't easily do, Claude claimed to have run it, but Anthropic's tools found "no evidence at all" that it had.[11]
- **It can't be held accountable; whoever uses it answers for its work.** British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal said Air Canada had, in effect, suggested its chatbot was "a separate legal entity that is responsible for its own actions", calling this "a remarkable submission".[14]
  It said Air Canada "is responsible for all the information on its website", whether from a static page or a chatbot.[14]
  The High Court said those who use AI for legal research have a professional duty to check it against authoritative sources.[10]

Whatever you call what it does, I'd assume it can't reliably tell when it's wrong, isn't aware of anything, and can't answer for its work.
So I'd treat it as a capable tool, not a colleague, and check its workings.
That's keeping a [human at the helm](ai-glossary.md): AI takes over tasks; people own the outcomes (see [Can AI replace our people?](../6-lead/can-ai-replace-our-people.md)).

## Three habits

I recommend three habits:

- **Brief it:** the purpose, the audience, the facts it needs and where its job stops.
- **Check it:** open its sources, and check harder when more is at stake.
- **Own it:** if it goes out under your name, it's yours.
