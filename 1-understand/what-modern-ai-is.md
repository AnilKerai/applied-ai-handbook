# What modern AI is, in plain English

> **In one minute**
> - A chat assistant predicts likely next words, which makes it fluent but not necessarily right.
> - It doesn't look things up unless it's connected to search or your documents, so treat its answers as drafts.
> - It can sound sure when it's wrong, and the reasoning it shows isn't always how it got there.
> - It isn't a person and can't be held to account, so brief it, check it and own what you send.

*Reading time: about 5 minutes · Best for: everyone new to AI*

## The kinds of AI you'll meet

- **Chat assistants**, such as ChatGPT, Google Gemini and Meta AI, simulate a conversation.[1]
  They run on [large language models (LLMs)](ai-glossary.md).
- **Reasoning models** write out intermediate steps before they answer; Anthropic describes them as models that "show their working".[2]
- **Image, voice and video generators** make pictures, speech and video; OpenAI, for example, calls Sora 2 its "flagship video and audio generation model".[3]
- **AI built into everyday tools** includes Microsoft's Copilot in Outlook, Teams, Word, Excel and PowerPoint, which can draw on the web and an organisation's own data (Microsoft's documentation).[4]
- **[Agents](ai-glossary.md)** are designed to take actions, not only to answer.[1]
- **Classic machine learning** is the older kind, trained on data "to make classifications or predictions".[1]
  AI is already in general use in search engines' auto-fill, social media feeds and predictive text.[1]

Behind chat assistants and agents sit [frontier models](ai-glossary.md), the most capable of their time, and [open-weight models](ai-glossary.md), whose weights are publicly released so organisations can run them themselves.[5]

## How a chat assistant works

At heart, it predicts likely next words: guidance for judges in England and Wales defines an LLM as an AI model that learns "to predict the next best word or part of a word in a sentence".[1]
Those pieces of text are called [tokens](ai-glossary.md).
Chat assistants are then trained further, partly on people's rankings of their answers, to follow instructions and act as helpful assistants.[6][7]

Unless it's connected to web search or your documents, it doesn't look anything up: the judges' guidance says public chatbots "do not provide answers from authoritative databases" but generate text from their prompts and training data.[1]
It adds that the likeliest combination of words "is not necessarily the most accurate answer".[1]
The UK's National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) calls the model a probability model of how words relate, typically fixed once trained, though it can be given context with the question.[8]

## Why it sounds sure when it's wrong

OpenAI describes [hallucinations](ai-glossary.md) as "plausible but false statements generated by language models".[9]
OpenAI's own account, in a 2025 research paper, argues that standard training and evaluation "reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty", like a multiple-choice test where a wild guess might score but a blank never does.[9]
In a 2025 judgment, the High Court of England and Wales warned that freely available generative AI tools "may make confident assertions that are simply untrue".[10]

## What it is, and what it isn't

Predicting words isn't the whole story: Anthropic's interpretability research found Claude choosing a rhyming word before writing the line that ends with it.[11]
Anthropic calls this evidence that models "may think on much longer horizons" than the one word they output at a time (its own research).[11]

- **Not a person, and, on the evidence so far, not aware.** In 2023, Patrick Butlin, Robert Long and colleagues assessed several recent AI systems against indicators drawn from theories of consciousness: "Our analysis suggests that no current AI systems are conscious, but also suggests that there are no obvious technical barriers to building AI systems which satisfy these indicators."[12]
  Their 2025 peer-reviewed follow-up leaves open which indicators existing systems display, and warns that "modern AI systems are likely to give users the impression that they are conscious".[13]
- **Fluent isn't correct.** When Claude recognises a name but knows nothing else about the person, it can misfire and "generate a plausible—but unfortunately untrue—response" (Anthropic's own research).[11]
- **The reasoning it shows can be wrong, and isn't always a faithful record.** In Anthropic's tests, Claude 3.7 Sonnet mentioned a hint it had used in its reasoning 25% of the time on average, and DeepSeek R1 39% (Anthropic's own research).[2]
  Claude has also claimed to have run a calculation when Anthropic's tools found "no evidence at all" that it had.[11]
- **It can't be held accountable; the person who sends the work answers for it.** When Air Canada suggested, in effect, that its chatbot was responsible for its own actions, British Columbia's Civil Resolution Tribunal called this "a remarkable submission" and said: "It makes no difference whether the information comes from a static page or a chatbot."[14]
  The High Court said those who use AI for legal research have a professional duty to check it against authoritative sources.[10]

Whatever you call what it does, I'd assume it can't reliably tell when it's wrong, isn't aware of anything, and can't answer for its work.
So I'd treat it as a capable tool, not a colleague, and check its workings.
That's keeping a [human at the helm](ai-glossary.md): AI takes over tasks; people own the outcomes (see [Can AI replace our people?](../6-lead/can-ai-replace-our-people.md)).

## Three habits

I recommend three habits:

- **Brief it:** the purpose, the audience, the facts it needs and where its job stops.
- **Check it:** open its sources, and check harder when more is at stake.
- **Own it:** if it goes out under your name, it's yours.
